BrainSail

Sail from A to B to rescue a stranded princess. When a storm hits, solve it with one random word.

Every storm is one rep that builds your lateral thinking muscles.

Welcome to the world of BrainSail

BrainSail is a small web game about sailing a boat across a sea map to rescue a stranded princess.

Storm clouds loop in circles between the boat at point A and the island at point B. You steer with the arrow keys and try to dodge them. When one hits, a Brainstorm opens: you get a problem and a few random words, and you use one word to spark a new, creative solution before the clock runs out. Then you decide if your answer is clear. If it is, the storm calms and you sail on. If not, the boat sinks and the level starts again.

The player's experience

Playful first, then a little pressure, then a small "oh!" when a silly word turns into a real idea. Three levels: more storms, less time, and fewer words to choose from.

Working title

BrainSail. Sail your brain around the storms.

Why this game

BrainSail is about Hallucination, which I define as believing there is only one way, or the traditional way, to solve a problem. The main idea is lateral thinking, Edward de Bono's name for looking at a problem from a new angle instead of going straight down the usual path. Each storm is one rep, and the game is built to develop those lateral thinking muscles.

Fun and play come first. Everything on this page, the lines, the colors, the motion, and the sounds, is chosen so a player under pressure still feels like they are playing, not being tested.

My Case Study

Hide and Luig

A free, hand-drawn point-and-click hide-and-seek game that runs in the browser. You uncover where Luig has hidden, level by level, by finding objects that unlock the next step. It is short, funny, and easy to replay, which made it a good game to study closely.

Three meaningful moments

Moment 1: stuck, then the clippers

Where was I?
When I learned the clippers cut the pumpkin, because for a few seconds I was stuck.
What I saw and heard
A door, plants, a fly, clippers, a pumpkin, a red arrow, a cat. Ethereal sounds that told me I had done something right.
What I did
I clicked the fly and it flew to the top left. I clicked around for any feedback and got nothing, until I clicked the ball at the top and heard a new sound that felt like success. I went through the left red arrow to the back of the outdoor closet, sent the fly back, and clicked the cat, which moved toward it. A box in the bottom right gave me clippers. At the front again, I clipped the pumpkin, it fell, and a hidden key was there. The key opens the door, where Luig is, and clicking him wins the level.
What changed
The angle of the scene changed, and the fly, cat, and pumpkin moved around.
What seemed possible
That if I kept clicking, I would figure it out.
What I understood
How to get the key to find Luig.

Moment 2: the door opens on Luig

Where was I?
The end of the level. I click the keyhole, the door opens, and Luig is sitting on a toilet!
What I saw and heard
Luig, sitting on a toilet inside the open door, in the same warm hand-drawn style.
What I did
I found the key inside the pumpkin with the clippers, then opened the door.
What changed
It was like a flipbook, where the animation unfolds as you click through.
What seemed possible
That I would win the level and move on to the next one!
What I understood
You have to find the key, or something, to reveal where Luig is hiding. The title made much more sense after this.

Moment 3: the beautiful robot

Where was I?
Revealing the next level with the beautiful robot.
What I saw and heard
A majestic robot in front of me.
What I did
I clicked around to find the next item to grab so I could eventually find Luig.
What changed
The level changed.
What seemed possible
That hidden objects would be inside the robot's arms or legs.
What I understood
There is comedy in this game. One reveal inside the robot was a chicken sitting on a ball for a bonus point, and another was clicking the robot's behind to reveal an object I needed to move forward.

What I learned from the research

  • Creator statement The maker frames the game as a hide-and-seek adventure and an absurd journey of curiosity and surprise (official page).
  • My observation I thought it was an object-chain puzzle: the fly, the cat, the clippers, the pumpkin, the key. I only saw the hide-and-seek once the door opened on Luig.
  • My interpretation Sound is the feedback language. A new sound meant I was right, and silence at the ear meant not yet. I think the maker wanted me to feel playful, curious, like an explorer, and to laugh.
  • Creator statement The one-item inventory is on purpose. A devlog for a later game says it forces planning and backtracking (Strange and Luig devlog). I did not confirm it for these levels.
  • My observation The bonus counter shows a second, optional goal: collect the bonus balls. One sat under a chicken inside the robot.
  • I wondered whether Luig is a spin on Luigi from Super Mario Bros., but I found no source supporting that connection. The evidence audit records it as unverified.

Two gaps I found in the AI's first reading

Gap 1: Perspective

The AI assumed the player knows from the start that they are hunting for Luig. My notes say otherwise: I thought it was an object-chain puzzle, and the title only made sense after the door opened.

My challenge to the AI is in the exchange below.

Gap 2: Interaction

The AI said the only goal is to find Luig. I found a second goal: the bonus balls, with a counter in the corner. The AI never asked what that second goal teaches.

"You said the only goal is to find Luig. That's not what I experienced. I clicked a ball and heard a new sound that told me I'd done something good, and a bonus counter showed up in the corner. Later, a chicken in the robot was sitting on a ball, and clicking it gave me a bonus point. So there is a second goal, finding the balls. What does a player learn from that second goal? How does it change the way I explore each scene?"

A short exchange, selected

Me

You described this game as a hide-and-seek where the player knows they are looking for Luig. That's not how it went for me. I thought I was solving a chain of puzzles with the fly, the cat, the clippers, the pumpkin, and the key. I didn't realize it was hide-and-seek until the door opened on Luig, and then the title finally made sense! The creator's devlog also says the one-item inventory forces planning and backtracking, which sounds like puzzle design to me. Please reconsider your reading using my observation and that source. What are two possible interpretations, and what evidence supports each? What evidence might weaken each, and what else should I look for in the game to decide?

AI

You're right, and the miss was real. My first reading assumed the player knows the goal from the start, and your notes show the opposite.

Reading A: a hide-and-seek game dressed as a puzzle chain. For: the creator frames it as hide-and-seek, each level ends in a comic reveal of Luig, and the title names the goal. Against: Luig does not appear until the end, and most of your play was object chaining.

Reading B: an object-chain puzzle that hides its goal until the end. For: your five linked steps and the dead end at the ear, plus the single item slot and the devlog about one-item inventories. Against: that devlog is about a sequel, and the creator describes the game as hide-and-seek, not a puzzle.

To decide, look for anything before the reveal that hints Luig is the target, check what the HELP button says, test whether you can hold two items, and ask a friend to play cold and say what they think the goal is.

Me, after thinking it over

Both readings can be true, in order. The first time through it is an object-chain puzzle, and the reveal flips it into hide-and-seek.

Three viewpoints, and the one that fit me

I asked the AI to play as a child, a game designer, and an artist. The designer saw a puzzle system and the artist saw a hand-drawn world that keeps you gentle even when you are stuck. The child's view felt closest to me.

"It's important the purpose for a game first and foremost to be fun, allow play to happen, and engaging to use. I have worked with kids before and they can be truly honest about that."

How the discussion changed my thinking

What did AI help me notice?
It helped provide more details faster from other sources, so I could do better pattern recognition, such as noticing that much of the experience is about sound and keeping it simple, one item at a time.
What did I challenge or correct?
The AI assumed the goal was obvious and that finding Luig was the only goal. I showed with my notes that I did not know the goal until the reveal, and that the bonus balls were a second goal.
What is my own conclusion?
Fun and play come first. A child would laugh at the chicken and chase the sounds before ever asking what the goal is. The meaning arrives after the play.
How will I use this?
In BrainSail the player starts by sailing and dodging, and the lateral thinking arrives through play: a random word that seems silly turns out to hold a real answer. I am also keeping what worked in Hide and Luig, a clear sound when you are right, a kind silence when you are not, and humor as the reward.

The Narrative and Concept

My interpretation of Hallucination

To me, a hallucination is believing there is only one way, or the traditional way, to solve a problem. It feels real, so we rarely question it. This game asks the player to question it.

The game world

You are sailing a small boat across a sea map, from point A to point B, where a princess is stranded on a palm-tree island waving for help. At first the water is calm. Then storms begin circling in the way. If a storm hits the boat, you have to solve its problem before time runs out, or the boat sinks.

The player's role

You are the hero in the boat, and your tool is lateral thinking. When a storm hits, a Brainstorm opens. The game gives you a random problem from a bank of problems and random words from a bank of words. You choose one word and use it to stimulate a new, creative solution before the timer ends. Both banks are random, so every playthrough is different.

When time is up, the game asks, "Is your answer clear?" and you choose yes or no. Yes calms the storm and you sail on toward B. No sinks the boat, and the same level starts again with a new problem and new words.

The experience I want to create

I want the player to feel the pressure of the storm, then the relief of a solution that came from an unexpected place. I hope they carry away practice in lateral thinking, and the sense that a random, even silly, word can hold a real answer.

How my references informed it

  • Hide and Luig reminded me that play comes first. Its hand-drawn scenes kept me playful even when I was stuck, sound told me I was right, and a surprise reveal was the reward. BrainSail's random word is my chicken sitting on a ball: a silly surprise that is secretly the answer.
  • Machinarium and Windosill are why the sea is hand-drawn, quiet, and a little surreal.
  • The overworld maps of classic SNES games are why the whole game is one map you steer across, with islands, wavy water, and a dotted route from A to B.
  • De Bono's lateral thinking is the skill. The random word is his random-entry idea turned into a game.

The Interaction and Player Journey

The flowchart

The journey in five beats

  1. Opening: Enter. A calm sea, a small boat at A, a stranded princess waving from B, and storm clouds drifting in circles between them. The clouds are the first clue, and they invite the player to steer around them.
  2. Stage 1: Notice. The first action is getting hit. A Brainstorm opens with a problem, three random words, a 1:30 clock, and a box to type in. The player learns that a hit is a problem to solve, not the end, and that a random word can open a new way in.
  3. Stage 2: Explore. The same action has a new consequence: two words, one minute, four storms. The player already trusts that a word can work, and now has to trust it with less choice and less time.
  4. Stage 3: Reconsider. Seven storms, one word, thirty seconds. With no choice of word, the familiar approach is gone. This is where the player discovers the "one right way" belief was the hallucination.
  5. Ending: Leave. The boat reaches B on calm water and the princess is rescued. The final thought is that a random word held a real answer. The player can sail again with new problems and words.

The storyboard, drawn by hand with Sharpies

  • Feedback The storms keep circling, so the danger is visible before anything happens.
  • Continues Keep sailing until a storm hits or I reach B.

  • Goal Survive the hit, then keep going toward B.
  • Input Pick one of the three words and type a solution.
  • Feedback The timer bar counts down. At the end the game asks, "Is your answer clear?"
  • Continues Yes calms the storm and the boat sails on. No sinks the boat and the level restarts.

  • How it develops Hallucination The "one right way" is gone. I have to use the one odd word I am given, and I discover it can still hold a real answer.
  • Continues Sail on, or the boat sinks and the level restarts.

The Style and Sound Guide

Visual

Hand-drawn Sharpie on paper

What I chose

Black outlines, scribbled storm clouds that flash with lightning, a small green boat, and A and B marked with dots. The whole game is a sea map you steer across, like the overworld maps in classic SNES games such as Super Mario World and Donkey Kong Country, with wavy water lines all over the sea. A hero rides the boat. A princess waits on the island at B, waving for help. Palm trees stand on both islands.

Why

A hand-drawn world feels like play, not a test, so solving problems under pressure stays fun. The clouds are readable at a glance.

Interface

One calm screen, big targets

What I chose

Steer with four keys: left, up, right, and down, drawn as triangles like the arrow keys on a MacBook Air. The storms are big, fast, and loop in wide circles, so getting around them takes timing. When the boat hits a storm, a second screen opens. The instructions sit at the top: use the word to stimulate a new creative solution or idea to tackle the problem. Under them are the problem, three word cards to pick from, and a real box to type in. A ring of marker ticks erases as time runs out. When time is up, the screen says "Time's up! Is your answer clear?" and only then shows the two buttons. The boat is the only green object, so the player always knows where they are.

Why

The player should understand what to do without instructions.

A playable sketch, not the finished game. Click the map, then use your arrow keys or press the triangles. Steer around the storms to the princess at B. If a storm hits, a second screen opens. The demo clock is 30 seconds, and the real Level 1 clock is 1:30. The problems and words come from a small sample bank.

Motion

Gentle, then silly

What I chose

Storms drift in wide, quick loops and flash with lightning. A hit gives a short shake. A solved storm puffs away like an erased scribble. The boat bobs. Sinking is a slow comic tilt with bubbles, not scary.

Why

Motion shows cause and effect, and keeps failure friendly.

Press a button to see how the game answers the player.

Audio

Playful and whimsical

What I chose

Marimba and ukulele plinks, bubble pops, and a soft water bed. Rain and thunder are the storm ambience: the closer a storm gets, the louder the rain, and every lightning flash comes with a rumble of thunder. A storm arrives with a silly low bloop. The last ten seconds tick like a woodblock. A good answer plays a rising xylophone chime. Sinking is a sliding whistle. Reaching B plays a short jingle.

Why

Sound confirms success and signals danger, and the calm sea is quiet so storms feel louder.

Sound stays off until you press a Hear it button.

My Two-Week Build Plan

Week 2

Build the playable prototype

  1. Days 1 to 2. The sea map: hand-drawn islands, wavy water, the boat, and steering with the arrow keys and on-screen triangles.
  2. Days 3 to 4. Storms that loop in circles with hit detection, then the Brainstorm screen: random problem and words from two banks, the timer, a typing box, and the "Time's up! Is your answer clear?" check.
  3. Days 5 to 6. Win and lose: yes calms the storm, no sinks the boat and restarts the level, reaching B rescues the princess. Levels 2 and 3 as settings for storms, time, and words.
  4. Day 7. Publish the prototype on my portfolio and play it start to finish.

The first version has

One full loop, three levels, about 20 problems and 40 words, simple sounds made in the browser, and the yes or no self-check.

Week 3

Test, refine, and publish

  1. Days 1 to 2. Playtest with about five people: classmates, a friend who is not a gamer, and a child, with a parent or guardian's permission. I will watch where they laugh, get stuck, and say "oh!".
  2. Days 3 to 4. Refine from what I see: storm speed and size, timers, the banks (cut confusing problems and words), instructions, and touch controls for phones.
  3. Days 5 to 6. Polish: the art pass (palms, hero, princess, lightning), the sound pass (rain and thunder ambience, chimes), and accessibility (keyboard, reduced motion, sound off by default).
  4. Day 7. Publish the final game, with a short note on what testers showed me and what I changed.

What I will ask my testers

  • Could you tell what the goal was without me explaining?
  • Did a random word give you an idea you would not have had?
  • Did the clock feel fun or like a test?
  • Did the yes or no check feel honest?

Keeping the scope achievable

  • No AI grading in version one. The player decides if their answer is clear, and can paste it into an AI chat for a second opinion.
  • No accounts and no saving.
  • Built with plain HTML, CSS, and JavaScript on GitHub Pages, with AI help.
  • If time runs short, I cut the lightning and ambience first and keep the core loop.

Done looks like: a stranger can start the game, steer, get hit, type an idea, and finish a level in about five minutes, and most testers say at least one random word gave them a new idea.
